❤️ _Behind the Closed Doors: Where Fear, Faith & Hope Meet_

There is something profoundly humbling about a hospital. Few places bring together so many human emotions under one roof — fear and courage, anxiety and anticipation, prayers and science, tears and smiles.

<!--more-->

_Outside an operating theatre, time seems to stand still._

![Hospital](https://d2coej5ollyd8p.cloudfront.net/tools/9b73caa7-17a4-46d1-b40a-12f6ec1a4395.png)

A husband sits with folded hands. A mother silently prays. A son walks restlessly down the corridor. Every time a door opens or a nurse walks past, a hundred anxious questions flash through their minds.

And then there is WhatsApp! A message gets posted on different groups:

_“The surgery has started. Please pray.”_

And within seconds messages start to pour in.

🙏 _Praying to God._
❤️ _Stay strong. We are with you._
🤞 _Please keep us updated._

Friends and relatives, some nearby and others miles away, keep checking their phones. They carry on with their work, attend meetings and go about their routines, but their minds keep returning to that little notification. They cannot enter the operating theatre or influence what happens inside. All they can do is wait, pray and remind the family that they are not alone.

Outside, people are fighting helplessness in their own way.

_Inside, the doctors are fighting a different battle._

Surgeons work with extraordinary concentration. Anaesthetists monitor vital signs. Nurses anticipate needs and move with precision. Every decision matters. They know a family is waiting outside, but for those crucial hours, their attention must remain entirely on the patient.

Hours pass. Fatigue sets in. Behind the masks are human beings who may have already spent an exhausting day caring for others. Yet they continue, drawing on their training, experience and commitment to give the patient the best possible chance.

Doctors are not miracle workers. They cannot promise every outcome. But they bring their knowledge, skill and every possible effort to the person lying on the operating table.

And sometimes, this journey begins long before the patient reaches the operating theatre.

It begins in a doctor's consultation room, with one diagnosis that changes the course of an entire family's life.

_CANCER._

Six letters. One word. Suddenly, life divides itself into a _before and an after._

Until yesterday, there were office meetings, holidays, family plans, children's futures and ordinary worries. Today, there are scans, biopsies, reports, consultations and unfamiliar medical terms.

_What stage is it? Has it spread? What are the treatment options? Will surgery be needed? What will life look like afterwards?_

The patient tries to absorb the doctor's words while the mind races ahead. The family tries to remain composed, even as the ground beneath them seems to shift.

A diagnosis changes more than a medical record. It changes routines, priorities, relationships and the way we look at the future. Work may take a back seat. Plans may need to be postponed. Everyday activities become complicated by appointments, treatment schedules, fatigue and recovery.

And somewhere in the middle of all these questions, an even more fundamental question emerges:

_Why does cancer have to exist at all? What good does it do?_

It is a question born not merely out of curiosity, but out of frustration, fear and a desperate search for fairness.

Why should a person who has spent a lifetime caring for others have to face this? Why should a young parent have to worry about being there for their children? Why should a family with so many dreams and plans suddenly have to reorganise its entire life around a diagnosis?

The uncomfortable truth is that _cancer does no good for the person who has it._ It is not a necessary process designed to benefit us, nor is it a punishment for anything we have done.

Our bodies are extraordinary biological systems. Trillions of cells divide, repair tissues and replace old cells to keep us alive. Our bodies have sophisticated mechanisms to repair damaged DNA, eliminate abnormal cells and protect us from disease. But these safeguards are not perfect.

Sometimes, changes accumulate in a cell, allowing it to escape the body's controls, multiply uncontrollably and, in some cases, spread to other organs. Age, inherited genetic factors, certain infections, tobacco, radiation and other exposures can contribute to cancer risk. Sometimes, it happens without any obvious preventable cause.

Life evolved with remarkable ways to sustain itself, but not with a guarantee of perfection or immortality.

And perhaps that is what makes cancer so difficult to accept. It doesn't distinguish between the kind and the unkind, the young and the old, the person with unfinished dreams and the person who has already accomplished so much.

_Biology does not promise fairness._

We want to believe that if we live well, work hard, care for others and make the right choices, life will protect us in return. But illness does not follow such rules.

Perhaps we don't need to invent a higher purpose for cancer or call it a blessing to find meaning in the way we respond to it.

The meaning can lie elsewhere — in the researchers working towards better treatments, the doctors striving to improve outcomes, the nurses comforting frightened patients, and the families and friends who refuse to let someone face the journey alone.

_The good is not in the cancer. The good is in what human beings do to fight it, treat it and care for one another through it._

Friends want to reassure us. They say, _“Stay positive.” “You are strong.” “Medical science has advanced so much.”_

And they mean every word.

Yet beneath the encouragement lies uncertainty. The patient may be frightened even while smiling. The family may appear strong while privately struggling to understand what lies ahead.

So people pray.

Some visit temples, churches or mosques. Some pray quietly at home. Some who have rarely prayed find themselves folding their hands again. Others place their faith in medical science, their doctors, the treatment and the people who care.

_WhatsApp becomes more than a tool. It becomes a circle of hope._

Someone shares a prayer. Someone recommends a doctor. Someone checks whether the patient has eaten. Another offers to accompany the family to an appointment. A friend who cannot be physically present makes sure to call.

And the updates continue:

_“The surgery went well.”_ — Relief.

_“The doctors have advised further treatment.”_ — Questions return.

_“The next scan is scheduled for next month.”_ — Anxiety finds its way back.

The journey from diagnosis to recovery is rarely a straight line. Depending on the condition, it may involve surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, medication, rehabilitation and repeated follow-up appointments. There are days of optimism and days when even the smallest task feels exhausting.

The end of one procedure may simply mark the beginning of another phase of care.

But somewhere along this journey, something begins to change — not necessarily the diagnosis, but the way we understand life.

Courage begins to mean showing up for another appointment despite the fear. It means asking questions, making difficult decisions and allowing others to help.

Families learn to celebrate smaller milestones: a reassuring report, a manageable day after treatment, a meal enjoyed without discomfort, a walk taken without assistance, or an evening spent laughing together without the illness occupying every conversation.

And slowly, _acceptance_ finds its place.

Not acceptance as surrender, but as an understanding that life may not return to exactly what it was before the diagnosis. Priorities may change. Plans may need to be reconsidered. New limitations may have to be navigated.

Yet life can still hold meaning, joy, ambition, laughter, relationships and possibilities.

_Acceptance is not giving up on the future. It is learning to live meaningfully while the future remains uncertain._

We can pursue the best available treatment, trust our doctors, pray for good outcomes and still acknowledge our fears. We can hope for recovery without pretending every day will be easy. We can appreciate the present without abandoning our dreams for tomorrow.

Perhaps that is what the operating theatre and the corridors outside it reveal about us.

In moments when we cannot control the outcome, we discover the importance of medical skill, human connection and the quiet courage of showing up for one another.

We often take our health, routines and loved ones for granted. Illness brings into focus the conversations we've postponed, the visits we've delayed and the gratitude we've forgotten to express.

It teaches us to be kinder, listen more carefully, and recognise that behind every medical report is a human being with dreams, fears, relationships and a life far bigger than the condition being treated.

A person with cancer is not merely a patient or a diagnosis. They remain a parent, a partner, a friend, a professional, a dreamer — a person whose identity and worth extend far beyond an illness.

_Cancer may change the course of a life, but it does not erase the value of that life._

Perhaps that is humanity at its best — always adapting, always fighting, but never giving up hope.

❤️ _Life may not remain the same after a diagnosis. It may ask us to change our plans, redefine our priorities and discover strengths we never knew we possessed. But even through uncertainty, surgery, treatment and recovery, there can still be room for hope, connection and new beginnings._

Perhaps we cannot control every chapter of our lives. We cannot always choose what happens to us, but we can choose how we face it — with courage, compassion, faith, the best available medical care and appreciation for the people who walk beside us.

We don't need to find a reason for every hardship. And we don't need to face every hardship alone.

_We may not always be able to change what life brings our way, but together, we can make the journey a little less frightening, a little more bearable and a little more hopeful._ ❤️
